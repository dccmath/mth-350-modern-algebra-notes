    Subrings.

    Definition 41 Subring.

    Let \(R\) be a ring, and let \(S\) be a subset of \(R\text{.}\) Then \(S\) is said to be a subring of \(R\) provided that \(S\) itself is a ring with the operations of addition and multiplication defined the same as in \(R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    As seen on the previous page - some properties of a ring are “inherited” for every subset of a ring \(R\text{.}\) This leads us to the subring test.
    🔗

    Theorem 42.

    Let \(R\) be a ring and let \(S\) be a subset of \(R\text{.}\) Then \(S\) is a subring of \(R\) if
    1. \(S\) is closed under addition
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(S\) is closed under multiplication
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(S\) contains \(0_{R}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(S\) is closed under additive inverses
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    The Subring Test ™ simplifies this even further, by combining closure under addition and closure under additive inverses into closure under subtraction. Also, if \(S\) is nonempty and closed under subtraction, then \(S\) contains \(0_{R}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 43 The Subring Test.

    Let \(R\) be a ring and let \(S\) be a subset of \(R\text{.}\) Then \(S\) is a subring of \(R\) if and only if
    1. \(S\) is nonempty
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(S\) is closed under subtraction
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(S\) is closed under multiplication
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Ideals.

    An ideal is a special kind of subring.
    🔗

    Definition 44 Ideal.

    An ideal \(I\) in a ring \(R\) is a subring of \(R\) such that \(rx\in I\) and \(xr\in I\) for all \(r\in R\) and \(x\in I\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    That is, if you multiply an element of \(R\) by an element of \(I\) (in any order), the element of \(R\) gets “sucked in” to \(I\text{.}\) So sometimes we say that ideals have the “sucking property”.
    🔗

    Field Extensions.

    The topic of field extensions is of central importance to much of abstract algebra. Many of the questions pertaining to field extensions have to do with finding solutions to polynomials. E.g., \(f(x) = x^{2} -2\in \mathbb{Q}[x]\) , but doesn’t have roots in \(\mathbb{Q}\) (that is no \(x\) for which \(f(x)=0\) ).
    🔗
    There are two roots for \(f(x)\) in \(\mathbb{R}\) : \(\sqrt{2}\) and \(-\sqrt{2}\) . But if we use the set \(\mathbb{Q}\cup \{\sqrt{2},-\sqrt{2}\}\) , then it’s not even a ring. So instead we define field extensions, like \(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})\) .
    🔗
    Define
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) = \{a + b\sqrt{2}: a, b\in \mathbb{Q}\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    15.

    Show \(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})\) is a subring of \(\mathbb{R}\text{,}\) and therefore is a ring.
    🔗
    🔗

    16.

    What do we still need to show that \(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})\) is a field? Prove these properties hold.
    🔗
    🔗

    17.

    Let \(F\) be a field. A subfield of \(F\) is a subring of \(F\) that is also a field. If \(F\) is a subfield of another field \(E\) then \(E\) is said to be a field extension (or simply an extension ) of \(F\text{.}\) How can \(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})\) be seen as a subfield? As a field extension? How can \(\mathbb{C}\) be seen as a field extension?

    Direct Sums.

    Definition 45 Cartesian product.

    Let \(R\) and \(S\) be rings. The Cartesian product of \(R\) and \(S\) is the set
    \begin{equation*} R\times S = \{ (r,s): r\in R \text{ and }s\in S\}. \end{equation*}
    The direct sum of \(R\) and \(S\) , denoted \(R\oplus S\) is the set \(R\times S\) with addition and multiplication defined componentwise - that is,
    \begin{equation*} (r_{1}, s_{1}) + (r_{2},s_{2}) = (r_{1} + r_{2}, s_{1} + s_{2}) \text{ and }(r_{1},s_{1})(r_{2},s_{2}) = (r_{1}r_{2},s_{1}s_{2}). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
