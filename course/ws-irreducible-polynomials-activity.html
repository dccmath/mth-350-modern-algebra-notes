    Worksheet Module 12: Roots, Factors, and Irreducible Polynomials
    

    Unbreakable
    🔗
    There are a lot of cool things about the way polynomials factor, or don’t factor. This module is a collection of “fun facts” (i.e., theorems) about how polynomial factoring works.
    🔗

    The Remainder Theorem.

    In Weekly Practice 11, we saw that, in \(\mathbb{R}[x]\) for \(f(x) = x^{2}-3x-2\text{,}\)
    • \(\displaystyle f(5) = 8\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle f(x)= (x-5)q(x)+8\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    1.

    Was it a weird coincidence that \(f(5)=8\) and that the remainder when \(f(x)\) was divided by \(x-5\) was \(8\text{?}\) Choose \(c\in\mathbb{R}\) and find \(f(c)\) and \(r(x)\) for which \(f(x)=(x-c)q(x)+r(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Let \(F\) be a field, \(c\in F\text{,}\) and \(f(x)\in F[x]\text{.}\) By the division algorithm we get for \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)=x-c\text{,}\) there exist unique polynomials \(q(x)\) and \(r(x)\) such that
    \begin{equation*} f(x) = (x-c)q(x)+r(x). \end{equation*}
    What are the restrictions on \(r(x)\) given by the division algorithm? Why does this mean \(r(x) = s\) for some \(s\in F\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    What happens when you evaluate the equation in (2) at \(x=c\text{.}\) Can you conclude whether or not the example in Weekly Practice 11 and (1) were a coincidence?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Here’s a formal statement of the remainder theorem, which will help us prove the factor theorem.
    🔗

    Theorem 60. The Remainder Theorem.

    Let \(F\) be a field, let \(p(x)\in F[x]\text{,}\) and let \(c\in F\text{.}\) The remainder wehn \(p(x)\) is divided by \((x-c)\) is equal to \(p(c)\text{.}\) That is, there exists a unique polynomial \(q(x) \in F[x]\) such that
    \begin{equation*} p(x) = (x-c)q(x) +p(c). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    The Factor Theorem.

    The factor theorem allows us to connect roots and factors. Here’s a couple definitions. Let \(R\) be a commutative ring and let \(p(x)\in R[x]\text{.}\)
    • An element \(c\in R\) is called a root of the polynomial \(p(x)\) provided that \(p(c) = 0_{R}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • A polynomial \(f(x)\) is a factor of the polynomial \(p(x)\) if \(f(x) \mid p(x)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 61. The Factor Theorem.

    Let \(F\) be a field, let \(p(x) \in F[x]\) and let \(c\in F\text{.}\) Then \(c\) is a root of the polynomial \(p(x)\) if and only if \((x-c)\) is a factor of \(p(x)\text{.}\)

    Theorem 62.

    A nonzero polynomial of degree \(n\in\mathbb{N}\) over a field has at most \(n\) roots in the field.
    🔗
    🔗
    There’s a proof of this in Investigation 10, page 147, Theorem 10.9, in your book. The proof is by induction.
    🔗
    🔗

    The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra (Factoring in \(\mathbb{C}[x]\)).

    Theorem 63. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra.

    Every polynomial of degree \(1\) or greater in \(\mathbb{C}[x]\) has a root in \(\mathbb{C}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    This means, every polynomial of degree \(n\ge 1\) in \(\mathbb{C}[x]\) has exactly \(n\) roots in \(\mathbb{C}\) (with repeated roots counted according to their multiplicities), and can be factored completely into linear factors. This factorization is unique up to associates, as you saw in the module prep.
    🔗
    🔗

    Generalizing the previous activity gives us the rational root theorem:
    🔗

    Theorem 64. The Rational Root Theorem.

    Let
    \begin{equation*} f(x) = a_{n}x^{n} + a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+ \cdots +a_{1}x + a_{0}\in \mathbb{Z}[x] \end{equation*}
    with \(a_{n}\neq 0\text{.}\) If \(r = \frac{p}{q}\in \mathbb{Q}\) is a root of \(f(x)\) with \(\gcd(p,q)=1\) then \(p\mid a_{0}\) and \(q\mid a_{n}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    There is one other well-known tool that is often used to determine irreducibility of polynomials in \(\mathbb{Q}[x]\)
    🔗

    Theorem 65. Eisenstein’s Criterion.

    Let \(f(x) = a_{n}x^{n} +a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+ \cdots + a_{1}x + a_{0}\in \mathbb{Z}[x]\) have degree \(n>1\text{.}\) If there is a prime \(p\) so that \(p\mid a_{i}\) for \(1\le i \le n-1\text{,}\) but \(p\nmid a_{n}\text{,}\) and \(p^{2}\nmid a_{0}\text{,}\) then \(f(x)\) is irreducible in \(\mathbb{Q}[x]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Use Eisenstein’s Criterion to answer the following.

    Show that for any prime \(p\) and any positive integer \(n\text{,}\) the polynomial \(f(x)=x^{n} + p\) is irreducible in \(\mathbb{Q}[x]\text{.}\) Conclude that there are irreducible polynomials in \(\mathbb{Q}[x]\) of any degree.

    Weekly Practice 12.

    1. Roots in \(\mathbb{C}[x]\) and \(\mathbb{R}[x]\).

    The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra says any polynomial of degree \(n \ge 1\) has a root in \(\mathbb{C}\text{.}\) When you use the quadratic formula to find the roots of \(f(x)\text{,}\) you sometimes get two roots that are in \(\mathbb{C}\backslash\mathbb{R}\text{,}\) which means that \(f(x)\) does not have a root in \(\mathbb{R}\text{.}\) Suppose \(z = a+bi\in \mathbb{C}\) is a root of \(f(x)\text{.}\) Then it turns out \(\overline{z}=a-bi\text{,}\) the complex conjugate of \(z\) is also a root of \(f(x)\text{.}\) (There’s a proof of this in Investigation 11, Theorem 11.6, on p. 157 in your book.)
    🔗

    (a)

    Suppose \(z = a+bi\in\mathbb{C}\) is a root of \(f(x)\text{.}\) Then \(\overline{z}= a-bi\) is also a root of \(f(x)\) (assume this without proof). Show that
    \begin{equation*} (x-z)(x-\overline{z}) \end{equation*}
    is a polynomial of degree \(2\) in \(\mathbb{R}[x]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Conclude that if \(f(x) \in \mathbb{R}[x]\) is an irreducible polynomial, then \(f(x)\) has degree \(1\) or \(2\text{.}\) (Use the fundamental theorem of algebra and part (a).)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Factoring in \(\mathbb{Z}_5\).

    To factor polynomials over fields other than \(\mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\text{,}\) trial and error is often the best option. We can first look for roots, which (by the Factor Theorem) give us linear factors. If we find roots, we can then divide by the corresponding linear factor to reduce the degree and look for roots again.
    🔗
    Use this process to factor
    \begin{equation*} f(x) = [3]x^{3} + [4]x^{2} + [3] \end{equation*}
    in \(\mathbb{Z}_{5}[x]\text{.}\)
