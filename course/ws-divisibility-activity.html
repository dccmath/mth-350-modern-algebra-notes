    The Leftovers
    🔗

    The Integers.

    We’ll assume the following about the integers as axioms. We don’t prove axioms, we use them as building blocks to prove other things. They give us a floor to stand on. Otherwise it’s turtles all the way down.
    🔗

    Axiom 2 Axioms of Integer Arithmetic.

    • The integers are closed under addition and multiplication, meaning that for all integers \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\) both \(a+b\) and \(ab\) are also integers.
      🔗
    • Addition and multiplication are commutative, meaning that for all integers \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\) \(a+b = b+a\) and \(ab = ba\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • Addition and multiplication are associative, meaning that for all integers \(a, b\) and \(c\text{,}\) \((a+b)+c = a+(b+c)\) and \((ab)c = a(bc).\)
      🔗
    • Multiplication distributes over addition, meaning that \(a(b+c) = ab+ac\) for all integers \(a, b,\) and \(c\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • The integer 0 is an additive identity, meaning that \(a+0 = a\) for every integer \(a\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • The integer 1 is a multiplicative identity, meaning that \(1a = a\) for every integer \(a\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • Every integer \(a\) has an additive inverse, typically denoted \(-a\text{;}\) in particular, \(a + (-a) = 0\) for every integer \(a\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Which axioms of a field are the integers missing?
    🔗
    🔗

    Divides.

    Definition 3 Divides.

    An integer \(a\) divides an integer \(b\text{,}\) denoted \(a\mid b\text{,}\) if there exists an integer \(q\) such that \(b=aq\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    In a popular high school mathematics textbook, students are told that one of the theorems in number theory is the following:
    🔗

    Theorem 4.

    If \(a,b,\) and \(c\) are integers where \(a\) is a factor of \(b\) and \(a\) is a factor of \(c\text{,}\) then \(a\) is a factor of \(b+c\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    1.

    Prove Theorem 4. It may help to translate this to use the word “divides”.
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Students are then asked about generalizing the theorem to:

    Theorem 5.

    If \(a,b,\) and \(c\) are integers where \(a\) is a factor of \(b\) and \(a\) is a factor of \(c\text{,}\) then \(a\) is a factor of \(bm+cn\) for all integers \(m\) and \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Is this true? Prove or disprove.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    The Division Algorithm.

    Theorem 6 The Division Algorithm.

    Let \(a\) and \(b\) be integers with \(a>0\text{.}\) Then there exist unique integers \(q\) and \(r\) such that
    \begin{equation*} b = aq + r \text{ and } 0\leq r <a\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We call \(q\) the quotient and \(r\) the remainder.
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Practice one more time: Come up with two integers \(a\) and \(b\) that satisfy the hypothesis of the division algorithm. (If you want a challenge, throw in a negative integer.) Trade these numbers with another group member and then apply the division algorithm to the numbers you receive.
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    What is different about applying the division algorithm when \(b<0\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    Do you think we should we assume the division algorithm as an axiom or try to prove it?
    🔗
    🔗

    6.

    Spoiler alert: We will prove it, since it turns out we can derive it from another, simpler axiom. To start, let’s do an example. Let \(a = 10\) and \(b=-58\text{.}\) Consider
    \begin{equation*} S = \{ x\in \mathbb{Z}: x\ge 0 \text{ and }x=b-am \text{ for some }m\in\mathbb{Z}\} . \end{equation*}
    Find some elements of \(S\text{.}\) What appears to be the least element of \(S\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    More Division Algorithm.

    7.

    If \(b\ge 0\) prove that \(b\in S\text{.}\) (Use the definition of the set \(S\text{.}\)) If \(b<0\text{,}\) is \(b\in S\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    8.

    We will take the following as an axiom:
    🔗

    Axiom 7 The Well-Ordering Principle.

    Every nonempty subset of the whole numbers contains a least element.
    🔗
    🔗
    Do you think \(S\) as defined earlier is always a nonempty subset of the whole numbers? What element can you be sure is in \(S\) if \(b<0\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Choose \(m=b\) and show that \(x=b-am\ge 0\) and so \(x\in S\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    9.

    At this point you are hopefully thinking that \(S\) is a nonempty subset of the whole numbers. So, we can apply the well-ordering principle and get a least element. We will show the least element is the remainder. So, let \(r\) be the least element of \(S\text{.}\) Since \(r\in S\text{,}\) we know there is an integer \(q\) such that \(r = b-aq\text{.}\) Thus \(b = aq + r\text{.}\) That’s good news!
    🔗
    We also need that \(0\le r < a\text{.}\) Why do we know \(0\le r\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    10.

    Now let’s show \(r<a\text{.}\) Suppose, for the sake of a contradiction that \(r\ge a\text{.}\) Show \(r-a\in S\text{.}\) (Recall \(r\in S\) and \(r=b-aq\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗

    11.

    You’ve reached a contradiction, how?
    🔗
    🔗

    12.

    What remains to be proven to show the division algorithm is that \(q\) and \(r\) is unique. You can find a partial proof near the end of Investigation 1, which will help you on a portfolio proof!
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Congruence.

    Definition 8 Congruence.

    Let \(n\) be a natural number, and let \(a\) and \(b\) be integers. Then \(a\) is congruent to \(b\) modulo \(n\), denoted \(a\equiv b \pmod{n}\text{,}\) provided that \(n\) divides \(a-b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Examples: Here’s a chance to remember congruences:
    🔗

    13.

    Give an example of two integers that are congruent modulo \(8\text{,}\) where one is positive and one is negative.
    🔗
    🔗

    14.

    Characterize all the integers \(m\) such that \(m\equiv 1 \pmod{3}\text{.}\) Then, what are all the integers \(m\) such that \(m\equiv 4 \pmod{3}\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    15.

    Prove: for all \(a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}\) and \(n\in \mathbb{N}\text{,}\) if \(a\equiv b \pmod{n}\) and \(c\equiv d \pmod{n}\) then \((a+c) \equiv (b+d) \pmod{n}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    16.

    Prove: for all \(a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}\) and \(n\in\mathbb{N}\text{,}\) if \(a\equiv b \pmod{n}\) and \(c\equiv d \pmod{n}\) then \(ac \equiv bd \pmod{n}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Fun with Congruence (and a connection to fields).

    17.

    Consider the set \(A = \{0,1,2,3,\dots, 7\}\) and define addition by \(x\oplus_{8} y = (x+y) \pmod{8}\) and multiplication by \(x\otimes_{8} y = xy \pmod{8}\text{.}\) (Here, “\(\pmod{8}\)” at the end means the remainder, aka the residue, of this number.)
    🔗
    Find the multiplicative and additive identities of \((A, \oplus, \otimes)\) (if they exist), and find the additive and multiplicative inverses of each element (if they exist). Will this be a field?
    🔗
    🔗

    18.

    Check digits: A UPC is a Universal Product Code, or the barcode on products. UPCs consist of 12 digits, and the last digit is known as a check digit. A check digit is there for error detection when a code is entered (they do this with your routing numbers for banks too). If the first 11 digits are \(x_{1}, x_{2},\dots, x_{11})\text{,}\) the check digit will be \(x_{12}\)
    \begin{equation*} x_{12}\equiv -(x_{1}, x_{2}, \dots, x_{11}) \cdot (3,1,3,1,3,1,3,1,3,1,3) \pmod{10} \end{equation*}
    where \(\cdot\) is the dot product, so multiply the entries in the same place, and then add up all the results. Verify that the check digit is correct on the box of Famous Amos cookies. Do the multiplication in your head using modular arithmetic!
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    19.

    My birthday was on a Thursday this year. Determine the next time my birthday will be on a Thursday again. (Use modular arithmetic! Also, leap years happen every 4 years, in years congruent to \(0\) mod \(4\text{.}\)) When will my birthday next be on a Sunday?
    🔗
    🔗

    Weekly Practice 2.

    1. Fun (?) with fields (?).

    Consider the set \(\{0,1,2,3,4,5,6\}\) and define addition by \(x\oplus_{7} y = (x+y) \pmod{7}\) and multiplication by \(x\otimes_{7} y = xy \pmod{7}\text{.}\) Find the multiplicative and additive identities of this number system (if they exist), and find the additive and multiplicative inverses of each element (if they exist). Is this set a field?
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Another way to talk about congruences.

    Consider the following theorem:
    🔗

    Theorem 9.

    Let \(n\) be a natural number, and let \(a\) and \(b\) be integers. Then \(a\equiv b \pmod{n}\) if and only if \(a\) and \(b\) yield the same remainder when divided by \(n\) (where the remainder is given by the Division Algorithm).
    🔗
    🔗

    (a)

    Do a couple examples of the theorem (in both directions!) below to gain a better understanding of what the theorem says. Show these examples and briefly explain how they illustrate the theorem.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Prove (at least) one direction of the theorem.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. UPCs!

    Find a product with a UPC and verify the check digit.
    🔗

    4. Another way congruences are weird.

    In high school algebra, linear equations, like \(5x+3 = 7\) always had one solution. Consider the set \(A = \{0,1,2,3\}\) and define addition by \(x\oplus_{4} y = (x+y) \pmod{4}\) and multiplication by \(x\otimes_{4} y = xy \pmod{4}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Show that in \((A,\oplus_{4}, \otimes_{4})\text{,}\) the equation \(2\otimes_{4} x \oplus_{4} 1 = 0\) has no solutions.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find a linear equation in the number system \((A,\oplus_{4}, \otimes_{4})\) that has exactly 2 solutions.
