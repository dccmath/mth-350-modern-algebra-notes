    Are polynomials just like integers?

    The Division Algorithm in \(F[x]\).

    5.

    We restrict talking about the division algorithm to \(F[x]\) where \(F\) is a field. When \(F\) is a field, \(F[x]\) is an integral domain. Why is \(F[x]\) never a field?
    🔗

    6.

    7.

    Why does \(F[x]\) for a field \(F\) not have zero divisors? (Hint: Consider the degree of the product of two polynomials.)

    8.

    Use Exercise 2 and Exercise 3 from the previous page to write statements that “look like” the division algorithm for integers (e.g., \(b=aq+r\text{,}\) where \(a = a(x)\) and \(b=b(x)\text{,}\) what are \(q(x)\) and \(r(x)\text{?}\)).
    🔗
    🔗

    9.

    In the division algorithm we had a restriction on the remainder \(r\text{:}\) \(0\le r < a\text{.}\) Based on your answer to the previous question, what would be a reasonable restriction on the remainder in the division algorithm in polynomials?
    🔗
    🔗

    10.

    The Division Algorithm for polynomials says: Let \(F\) be a field and let \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\) be polynomials in \(F[x]\) with \(g(x)\neq 0\text{.}\) Then there exist unique polynomials \(q(x)\) and \(r(x)\) in \(F[x]\) such that
    \begin{equation*} f(x)=g(x)q(x)+r(x) \end{equation*}
    where \(r(x)=0\) or \(\deg(r(x)) < \deg(g(x))\text{.}\)
    🔗
    What are the similarities and differences with the division algorithm for integers?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Greatest Common Divisor.

    11.

    Suppose in \(\mathbb{R}[x]\) we have \(f(x)=2x^{2}+x-6\) and \(g(x)=2x^{2}-7x+6\text{.}\) Note that we have the following
    • \(f(x)=(x+2)(2x-3)\) and \(g(x)=(x-2)(2x-3)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(f(x)=(2x+4)(x-\frac{3}{2})\) and \(g(x) = (2x-4)(x-\frac{3}{2})\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(f(x) = \left(\frac{1}{2}x+1)(4x-6)\right)\) and \(g(x)=\left(\frac{1}{2}x-1\right)(4x-6)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Based on this, what are some common divisors of \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    12.

    If we want the greatest common divisor to be unique, which common divisor should we pick?
    🔗
    🔗

    13.

    Here’s a definition:
    🔗

    Definition 59.

    Let \(F\) be a field and let \(f(x), g(x)\in F[x]\) that are not both zero. The greatest common divisor of \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\) is the polynomial \(d(x)\in F[x]\) that satisfies the following three conditions:
    • \(d(x)\) divides \(f(x)\) and \(d(x)\) divides \(g(x)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(h(x)\in F[x]\) and \(h(x)\) divides both \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\) then \(h(x)\) divides \(d(x)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(d(x)\) is a monic polynomial (i.e., the leading coefficient is \(1_{F}\) )
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    What are the similarities and differences between this definition and the integer definition?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Factoring polynomials.

    In this module we’ve explored how polynomials are like integers. We’ve explored factoring polynomials a bit (and you likely explored it a lot in high school). Let’s explore some more.
    🔗

    (a)

    Let \(f(x)=x^{2}-3x-2\) be in \(\mathbb{R}[x]\text{.}\) Notice that \(f(5)=8\text{.}\) What is the remainder when \(f(x)\) is divided by \(x-5\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Let \(g(x) = x^{2}-3x-10\) be in \(\mathbb{R}[x]\text{.}\) Determine \(g(5)\) and determine the remainder when \(g(x)\) is divided by \(x-5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Let \(h(x)=x^{2}-3x-10\) be in \(\mathbb{Q}[x]\text{.}\) Determine \(h(-2)\) and determine the remainder when \(h(x)\) is divided by \(x-(-2)\text{.}\)
