    Definitions.

    Definition 21.

    A ring is a set \(R\) together with two binary operations, called addition (\(+\)) and multiplication (\(\cdot\)), such that all of the following axioms hold:
    🔗
    • The set \(R\) is closed under addition and multiplication, meaning that for all \(x, y \in R\text{,}\) \(x+y \in R\) and \(x \cdot y \in R\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Addition is associative, meaning that for all \(x, y, z \in R\text{,}\) \((x+y)+z = x+(y+z)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Addition is commutative, meaning that for all \(x, y \in R\text{,}\) \(x +y = y+x\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The set \(R\) contains an additive identity, also called a zero element, meaning that there exists some element \(0_{R} \in R\) such that \(x+0_{R} = x\) for all \(x \in R\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Every element of \(R\) has an additive inverse within \(R\), meaning that for every \(x \in R\text{,}\) there exists \(y \in R\) such that \(x + y = 0_{R}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Multiplication is associative, meaning that for all \(x, y, z \in R\text{,}\) \((x \cdot y) \cdot z = x \cdot (y \cdot z)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Multiplication distributes over addition, meaning that for all \(x, y, z \in R\text{,}\) \(x \cdot (y+z) = x \cdot y + x \cdot z\) and \((x+y) \cdot z = x \cdot z + y \cdot z\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Polynomials.

    Definition 22.

    Let \(R\) be a commutative ring. A polynomial in \(x\) over \(R\) is an expression of the form
    \begin{equation*} a_{n}x^{n} + a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+ \cdots a_{2}x^{2} + a_{1}x^{1} + a_{0}x^{0}, \end{equation*}
    where \(n\) is a nonnegative integer and \(a_{n},a_{n-1},\dots,a_{2},a_{1},a_{0}\) are elements of \(R\text{.}\) The polynomial ring over \(R\) is the set of all polynomials over the ring \(R\text{,}\) and is denoted \(R[x]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Back to units and zero divisors in \(\mathbb{Z}_n\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Consider \(\mathbb{Z}_{24}\text{.}\) Since 16 and 24 are not relatively prime, it should follow that \([16]_{24}\) is a zero divisor in \(\mathbb{Z}_{24}\text{.}\) Let’s show \([16]_{24}\in \mathbb{Z}_{24}\) is a zero divisor. Find \(d=\gcd(16,24)\text{.}\) Show \(b=\frac{24}{d}\) is a nonzero integer, and \([16]_{24}[b]_{24}= [0]_{24}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Generalize what you did in part (a) to \(\mathbb{Z}_{n}\text{,}\) thus showing that if \(\gcd(a,n)>1\) then \([a]_{n}\) is a zero divisor in \(\mathbb{Z}_{n}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Now, assume \([a]_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}\) is a unit. Let’s show \(\gcd(a,n)=1\text{.}\) Since \([a]_{n}\) is a unit, there exists \([b]_{n}\in \mathbb{Z}_{n}\) such that \([a]_{n}[b]_{n} = [1]_{n}\text{.}\) This can be rewritten as \([ab]_{n} = [1]_{n}\text{.}\) This means \([ab]_{n}\) and \([1]_{n}\) are equal as congruence classes.
    🔗
    First, recall this Lemma from Module 3: Lemma 12
    🔗
    Apply the Lemma (here \(\sim\) is congruence modulo \(n\) ), and then work with what you get to show that there’s a linear combination of \(a\) and \(b\) equal to \(1\text{.}\) Then conclude that \(\gcd(a,n)=1\text{.}\)
