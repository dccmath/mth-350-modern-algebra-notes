    Rings.

    Here’s a reminder of the ring axioms which we will use in this module!
    🔗

    Definition 24.

    A ring is a set \(R\) together with two binary operations, called addition (\(+\)) and multiplication (\(\cdot\)), such that all of the following axioms hold:
    🔗
    • The set \(R\) is closed under addition and multiplication, meaning that for all \(x, y \in R\text{,}\) \(x+y \in R\) and \(x \cdot y \in R\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Addition is associative, meaning that for all \(x, y, z \in R\text{,}\) \((x+y)+z = x+(y+z)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Addition is commutative, meaning that for all \(x, y \in R\text{,}\) \(x +y = y+x\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The set \(R\) contains an additive identity, also called a zero element, meaning that there exists some element \(0_{R} \in R\) such that \(x+0_{R} = x\) for all \(x \in R\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Every element of \(R\) has an additive inverse within \(R\), meaning that for every \(x \in R\text{,}\) there exists \(y \in R\) such that \(x + y = 0_{R}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Multiplication is associative, meaning that for all \(x, y, z \in R\text{,}\) \((x \cdot y) \cdot z = x \cdot (y \cdot z)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Multiplication distributes over addition, meaning that for all \(x, y, z \in R\text{,}\) \(x \cdot (y+z) = x \cdot y + x \cdot z\) and \((x+y) \cdot z = x \cdot z + y \cdot z\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Here’s a list of theorems. If we can prove these using ring axioms, we know they are true about all rings including - square matrices \(\mathcal{M}_{n\times n}\text{,}\) integers \(\mathbb{Z}\text{,}\) even integers \(\mathbb{E}\text{,}\) the rational numbers \(\mathbb{Q}\text{,}\) polynomials \(R[x]\) for a commutative ring \(R\text{,}\) the real numbers \(\mathbb{R}\text{,}\) the complex numbers \(\mathbb{C}\text{,}\) and anything else we can show is a ring.
    🔗

    Theorem 25.

    (Additive Cancellation) Let \(R\) be a ring. For all \(x,y,z\in R\text{,}\) if \(x+z = y+z\) then \(x=y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 26.

    (Multiplication by \(0\) ) Let \(R\) be a ring and let \(0\) be the additive identity of \(R\text{.}\) Then \(0x=0=x0\) for all \(x\in R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 27.

    (Uniqueness) Let \(R\) be a ring.
    • (Additive identities) Suppose that both \(0\) and \(0'\) are zero elements for \(R\text{.}\) Then \(0=0'\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • (Additive inverses) Let \(x\in R\text{.}\) Suppose that both \(y\) and \(y'\) are additive inverses for \(x\text{.}\) Then \(y=y'\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • (Multiplicative identities) Suppose that both \(1\) and \(1'\) are both identities for \(R\text{.}\) Then \(1=1'\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • (Multiplicative inverses) Let \(x\in R\text{.}\) Suppose that both \(y\) and \(y'\) are multiplicative inverses for \(x\text{.}\) Then \(y=y'\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 28.

    (Fields are integral domains.) Let \(R\) be a ring with identity and let \(x\in R\) be a unit. Then \(x\) is not a zero divisor.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 29.

    (Finite integral domains are fields.) Let \(R\) be a finite integral domain. Let \(a\in R\) with \(a\neq 0\text{.}\) Then \(a\) is a unit.
    🔗
    🔗
    In the following theorem note that the symbol \(-x\) means “the additive inverse of \(x\)”. We define subtraction in terms of addition, e.g., \(x-y = x + (-y)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 30.

    (The negatives.) Let \(R\) be a ring, and let \(x,y,z \in R\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    • \(\displaystyle -(-x) = x\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle x(-y)=-(xy) = (-x)y\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle (-x)(-y) = xy\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle x(y-z) = xy-xz\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle (y-z)x= yx-zx\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle -(x+y)=-x-y\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 31.

    (Generalized Distributive Law) Let \(R\) be a ring, let \(n\ge 2\text{,}\) and let \(a,b_{1},b_{2},\dots, b_{n}\in R\text{.}\) Then
    • \(\displaystyle a(b_{1}+b_{2}+\cdots+b_{n})=ab_{1}+ab_{2}+\cdots+ab_{n}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle (b_{1}+b_{2}+\cdots+b_{n})a = b_{1}a+b_{2}a+\cdots+b_{n}a.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Let’s prove some theorems!

    1.

    In Weekly Practice 1 you proved in any field \(F\) and any \(a\in F\text{,}\) \(0_{F}\cdot a = 0_{F}\text{.}\) You actually only needed the ring axioms. Let’s recall that proof.
    🔗

    Theorem 32.

    (Multiplication by \(0\) ) Let \(R\) be a ring and let \(0\) be the additive identity of \(R\text{.}\) Then \(0x=0=x0\) for all \(x\in R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Prove it! Hint: Consider \((0+0)x\) in two different ways.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    In Problem 5 of the proof portfolio, you’ll prove additive and multiplicative cancellation holds in fields. For additive cancellation, you only need the ring axioms. Let’s prove multiplicative cancellation holds in more than just fields.
    🔗

    Theorem 33.

    (Multiplicative Cancellation) Let \(R\) be a ring and let \(z\) be a nonzero element of \(R\) that is not a zero divisor. For \(x,y\in R\text{,}\) if \(xz=yz\text{,}\) then \(x=y\text{.}\) Similarly if \(zx = zy\) then \(x=y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Prove it! Hint: Starting with \(xz=yz\text{,}\) add \((-y)z\) to both sides.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We established in the preview activity for this module that every field is an integral domain. It turns out every finite integral domain is a field.
    🔗

    Theorem 34.

    (Finite integral domains are fields.) Let \(R\) be a finite integral domain. Let \(a\in R\) with \(a\neq 0\text{.}\) Then \(a\) is a unit.
    🔗
    🔗
    Prove it! Okay, this one has a trick, so let’s walk through some parts.
    🔗

    3.

    Since \(R\) is an integral domain, \(R\) has identity. Consider the set \(A\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} A := R- \{0\} = \{1, a_{1},a_{2},\dots, a_{n}\}. \end{equation*}
    Let \(a\in R\) with \(a\neq 0\text{.}\) Multiply each element of \(R-\{0\}\) by \(a\) to get
    \begin{equation*} B := \{a,aa_{1},aa_{2},\dots, aa_{n}\}. \end{equation*}
    Explain why all elements of \(B\) are unique. (Go by contradiction - suppose for some \(i,j\text{,}\) \(aa_{i} = aa_{j}\text{.}\) Then what?
    🔗
    🔗

    Why does this imply that \(a\) has an inverse?

    Theorem 35.

    Let \(R\) be a ring and let \(x,y\in R\text{.}\) Then \(x(-y) = -(xy)\text{.}\)

    Weekly Practice 6.

    1. Matrix problems.

    Prove \(\mathcal{M}_{2\times 2}(\mathbb{R})\) (two by two matrices with entries from the real numbers) has a zero divisor. What reason besides having zero divisors is \(\mathcal{M}_{2\times 2}(\mathbb{R})\) not an integral domain?
    🔗
    🔗

    2. A negative result.

    We showed (or assume we showed) that for a ring \(R\) and \(x,y\in R\text{,}\) we have \(x(-y) = -(xy)=(-x)y\text{.}\) Consider the following proof that \(xy =(-x)(-y)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Proof: From \(x(-y)=-(xy)=(-x)y\) we obtain \((-x)(-y) = x[-(-y)] = xy\text{,}\) since in any ring the inverse of the inverse of an element is the original element.
    🔗
    Fill in the details. Then use the result to prove that if \(u\) in \(R\) is a unit, then \(-u\) is also a unit.
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Yay, induction!

    Prove the generalized distributive law by induction. Let \(R\) be a ring, let \(n\ge 2\text{,}\) and let \(a,b_{1},b_{2},\dots, b_{n}\in R\text{.}\) Then \(a(b_{1}+b_{2}+\cdots+b_{n})=ab_{1}+ab_{2}+\cdots+ab_{n}\text{.}\)
